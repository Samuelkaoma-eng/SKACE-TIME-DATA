Abstract
Paradata provides valuable insights into respondent and enumerator behavior during survey
data collection, yet it remains underutilized due to the complexity of transforming raw audit
logs into meaningful analytical features. This project proposes the design and implementation
of an automated system for extracting timing-based and behavioral features from survey
paradata generated by SurveyCTO and ODK platforms. The system ingests raw audit logs,
parses platform-specific formats into a common intermediate representation, and applies a
structured feature extraction pipeline to compute over thirty timing-based indicators spanning
question-level, section-level, and survey-level granularities. Statistical measures (including
entropy, variance, skewness, and kurtosis) characterize response distributions, while
Interquartile Range (IQR) thresholding and rule-based logic identify anomalous interview
patterns. Validation is conducted using synthetic datasets with injected anomalies, evaluated
against precision, recall, and effect-size criteria. The outcome is a reproducible Python feature
library, accompanying paradata format documentation, and an empirical validation report,
providing a practical bridge between theoretical survey methodology research and scalable
quality assurance implementation.
1. Introduction
The quality of survey data plays a critical role in research, policy formulation, and decision-
making across public health, economics, and the social sciences [1]. Digital data collection
platforms such as SurveyCTO and Open Data Kit (ODK) have substantially improved the
efficiency of field survey administration. Both platforms automatically record auxiliary data,
commonly referred to as paradata, that captures detailed information about how interviews
are conducted rather than the responses themselves [2].
Paradata includes event-level timestamps, navigation sequences, inter-keystroke intervals,
pause durations, and back-navigation events logged throughout the interview process [4].
These behavioral indicators offer diagnostic potential that extends well beyond conventional
quality checks. Unusually fast completion times may indicate rushed or fabricated responses;
extended pauses may signal confusion, enumerator distraction, or external interruptions; and
atypical edit patterns may point to satisficing or straight-lining behavior [3, 5, 13].
Despite this potential, paradata is routinely underutilized in practice [4]. Raw audit logs from
SurveyCTO and ODK differ in structure, field naming conventions, and granularity, making cross-
platform analysis non-trivial. Existing quality assurance approaches largely depend on aggregate
indicators such as total interview duration or manual back-check supervision, methods that are
poorly equipped to detect subtle behavioral irregularities at scale [6, 7].
This project addresses these challenges by developing an automated, platform-agnostic
pipeline that extracts, structures, and analyses timing-based paradata features. By combining
established data engineering techniques [8] with statistical anomaly detection methods [9, 10],
the system transforms raw logs into structured datasets suitable for quality assessment,
operationalizing a body of survey methodology research that has hitherto remained largely
theoretical [4, 6]. The use of synthetic data generation for validation is further motivated by its
role as a controlled and reproducible approach to benchmarking detection systems in the
absence of fully labelled real-world corpora [11, 14].
1.1 Problem Statement
Existing survey quality assurance methods are limited in their ability to detect complex patterns
of poor data collection [1, 13]. Most approaches rely on simple aggregate metrics (primarily
total interview duration) that do not adequately capture behavioral irregularities during the
interview process. Consequently, issues such as rushed responses, inattentive navigation, or
fabricated data entry may go undetected [6].
Although paradata contains rich behavioral information, it is stored in raw audit logs whose
format varies across platforms. SurveyCTO produces event-based CSV logs with millisecond-
level timestamps tied to field identifiers, whereas ODK logs differ in event taxonomy and timing
granularity [4]. The absence of automated, format-aware tools for transforming these logs into
analytical features creates a significant barrier for researchers and survey organizations seeking
to operationalize paradata at scale [7]. Furthermore, the statistical methods required to identify
anomalous timing behavior must be robust to the non-normal distributions characteristic of
response time data [9, 12], underscoring the need for a principled and well-justified detection
approach.
1.2 Project Aim
The aim of this project is to design and implement an automated pipeline for extracting,
cataloguing, and analyzing timing-based paradata features from SurveyCTO and ODK audit logs,
in order to support scalable survey data quality assessment.

2. Literature Review
The Total Survey Error (TSE) framework, as developed by Groves et al. [1], provides the
theoretical foundation for understanding data quality as the product of error sources spanning
all stages of the survey process. Within this framework, paradata has emerged as a practical
mechanism for monitoring and diagnosing error at the interviewing stage. Couper [2]
demonstrated that process data captured within computer-assisted survey instruments could
serve as quality indicators, establishing the methodological basis for timing-based analysis.
Yan and Tourangeau [5] directly examined response latency as a quality indicator, finding that
question complexity, respondent age, and familiarity with topic all predicted response time
variation. Their work identified mean response latency and within-respondent latency variance
as particularly discriminative features. Questions answered unusually quickly relative to their
complexity profile are associated with reduced cognitive engagement and lower data quality.
This finding directly motivates the inclusion of question-level response time, time-to-first-
interaction, and complexity-adjusted pace features in the proposed taxonomy.
Conrad, Tourangeau, and Couper [3] explored adaptive survey design using paradata,
demonstrating that real-time behavioral signals (including extended pauses and repeated back-
navigation) could identify respondent difficulty and trigger adaptive interventions. Their
analysis underscored the relevance of section-level pause frequency, within-section timing
variance, and back-navigation rate as indicators of processing difficulty or disengagement.
The most directly applicable empirical work for the present project is that of Wenz, Kreuter,
and Janke [6], who investigated data fabrication detection using paradata in the context of
face-to-face interviewer-administered surveys. They found that fabricated interviews exhibited
characteristically compressed response time distributions (notably reduced inter-quartile
spread and lower variance in question-level durations) along with abnormally low back-
navigation counts and unusually uniform section pace. These findings establish the statistical
basis for the anomaly detection component of this project and directly inform the selection of
IQR-based thresholding as a detection method.
Kreuter [4] provides a broader treatment of paradata-based quality improvement, surveying
implementations across several large-scale survey programs. A recurring finding is that
computational processing of timing data requires platform-specific parsing strategies before
any analytical features can be extracted, a practical challenge that motivates the parser
development component of this project. Survey-level features such as total interview duration,
speed trajectory across the interview, and time-of-day effects are identified by Kreuter [4] as
robust indicators that survive aggregation across diverse survey instruments.
Taken together, this body of work establishes a clear empirical basis for a taxonomy of timing-
based features organized across three levels of granularity. Table 1 maps the key findings from
prior work to the features planned for extraction in this project.

3. Proposed Methodology
The project follows an experimental and iterative development methodology. The system is
structured as a multi-stage pipeline progressing from raw log ingestion through parsing, feature
extraction, statistical characterization, anomaly detection, and output generation.
Fig. 1. System pipeline for paradata feature extraction and anomaly detection.
Figure 1 illustrates the modular pipeline architecture, highlighting the separation between the
parsing layer and the feature extraction layer. Raw audit logs from SurveyCTO and ODK enter at
the left; platform-specific parsers normalize them into a Common Intermediate Representation
(CIR); and the feature extraction, statistical characterization, and anomaly detection modules all
operate exclusively on this standardized format. This separation ensures that adding support
for a new platform requires only a new parser, without modifying downstream components.
The pipeline begins with the ingestion of raw audit logs from SurveyCTO and ODK. These logs
differ substantially in format: SurveyCTO produces event-based CSV files with millisecond-
precision timestamps tied to field identifiers and event types (e.g., form-start, form-resume,
constraint, end), while ODK logs record a different event taxonomy with coarser timing
granularity and distinct field naming conventions.
The parser module will implement a class hierarchy in which a base ParadataParser defines the
common intermediate schema and shared transformation logic, while subclasses
SurveyCTOParser and ODKParser handle platform-specific ingestion and timestamp
normalisation. The resulting intermediate representation is a standardised Pandas DataFrame
with columns: interview_id, field_name, event_type, start_ms, end_ms, and duration_ms. All
downstream feature extraction operates exclusively on this intermediate format, ensuring that
new platform parsers can be added without modifying the feature library.
Feature engineering is applied to the intermediate representation to compute timing-based
indicators across three levels of granularity. At the question level, the system calculates
response time per field, time-to-first-interaction, edit count, and whether the field was
revisited. At the section level, it aggregates section pace, within-section timing variance, pause
frequency, and pause duration. At the survey level, it computes total interview duration, speed
trajectory across the interview arc, back-navigation rate, and time-of-day flags. Statistical
measures (including variance, entropy, skewness, and kurtosis) characterize the distributional
properties of each feature across questions and across interviews.

3.2 Parser Design and Format Handling
Both SurveyCTO and ODK are within scope, and their audit log formats require distinct parsing
strategies before a unified analysis can proceed. SurveyCTO logs are event-based CSV files in
which each row records a single form event with fields for event (e.g., form-start, form-resume,
constraint, end), node (the XPath field identifier), and start and end timestamps in epoch
milliseconds. ODK audit logs record event, node, and start and end fields in a broadly similar
CSV structure, but with a different event taxonomy (using terms such as “question” rather than
field-specific events) and timestamps in ISO 8601 format rather than epoch milliseconds.
The parser module will implement a class hierarchy in which a base ParadataParser defines the
common intermediate schema and shared transformation logic, while subclasses
SurveyCTOParser and ODKParser handle platform-specific ingestion and timestamp
normalisation. The resulting intermediate representation is a standardised Pandas DataFrame
with columns: interview_id, field_name, event_type, start_ms, end_ms, and duration_ms. All
downstream feature extraction operates exclusively on this intermediate format, ensuring that
new platform parsers can be added without modifying the feature library.
3.3 Anomaly Detection
Definition of an Anomaly
For the purposes of this project, an anomaly is defined as an interview exhibiting statistically
significant deviation from expected timing behavior, including one or more of the following
criteria:
 Extremely low or high total duration relative to the sample IQR (below Q1 minus 1.5
times IQR or above Q3 plus 1.5 times IQR)
 Abnormally low variance in response times, indicating suspiciously uniform pacing
across questions of differing complexity
 Absence of natural pauses or back-navigation across more than twenty consecutive
questions
 Inconsistent timing patterns relative to question complexity, specifically uniformly fast
responses irrespective of question length or type
Anomaly detection is performed at the interview level using two complementary approaches.
Statistical flagging applies IQR-based thresholding: for each timing feature, interviews with
values below Q1 minus 1.5 times IQR or above Q3 plus 1.5 times IQR are flagged as potential
anomalies. This is consistent with the fabrication detection approach validated by Wenz et
al. [6], whose key discriminative features (compressed response time IQR and low back-
navigation) are directly captured in the feature taxonomy.
The choice of IQR-based detection is motivated by its robustness to non-normal distributions,
which are common in timing-based behavioral data [9, 12]. Unlike Z-score methods, which
assume approximate normality and are therefore sensitive to extreme values in skewed
distributions [12], IQR-based thresholding makes no distributional assumption and is resistant
to the influence of outliers on the threshold boundaries themselves. This property is
particularly important for response time data, which typically exhibits right-skewed
distributions with heavy tails [5]. While modified Z-score methods (based on the median
absolute deviation) offer a comparable degree of robustness [12], IQR thresholding is preferred
here for its direct interpretability within the survey quality monitoring context and its
established use in paradata-based fabrication detection [6]. The project intentionally avoids
machine learning-based approaches to maintain interpretability and alignment with survey
methodology practices, where transparent and explainable quality indicators are preferred over
black-box models. While robust, IQR-based methods may fail to detect multivariate anomalies
where individual features appear normal but joint patterns are irregular, a limitation
acknowledged in Section 8.
Fig. 2. Illustration of outlier detection using the boxplot (IQR) method.
Figure 2 illustrates how the IQR method identifies outlying interviews: the central box spans Q1
to Q3, the whiskers extend to 1.5 times IQR on each side, and points beyond the whiskers are
flagged as statistical anomalies. This method is applied independently to each timing feature,
with interviews receiving an anomaly score proportional to the number of features on which
they are flagged.
Rule-based flagging supplements statistical detection with domain-derived thresholds:
interviews where total duration falls below a minimum plausible completion time, where back-
navigation rate is zero across more than twenty questions, or where the timing entropy of the
response sequence falls below a defined floor are automatically flagged. These rules
operationalize the qualitative indicators described by Kreuter [4] and Conrad et al. [3].
3.3.1 Computational Complexity Considerations
The pipeline is designed with computational efficiency in mind. Feature extraction operations
use vectorized Pandas operations with a time complexity of approximately O(n) per interview,
where n is the number of audit log events, ensuring scalability to large datasets. The IQR-based
anomaly detection step operates in O(m log m) time over m interviews due to the sorting
requirement, which remains tractable at the scale of typical survey datasets (hundreds to low
thousands of interviews). Chunked processing will be applied for datasets exceeding available
memory thresholds, with benchmarking conducted on synthetic datasets of increasing size (500,
2,000, and 10,000 simulated interviews) to characterize throughput and identify bottlenecks
prior to deployment.

5. Evaluation Methodology
Evaluation will proceed at two levels: feature validation and anomaly detection performance.
Feature validation will assess whether individual features are statistically discriminative. For
each feature, Mann-Whitney U tests will compare distributions between normal and
anomalous interviews in the synthetic dataset. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) will be reported.
Features with non-significant separation or negligible effect size will be documented and
excluded from the anomaly detection stage, with the rationale recorded in the feature
documentation.
Anomaly detection performance will be assessed against labelled ground truth in the synthetic
dataset. The primary metrics are precision (proportion of flagged interviews that are true
anomalies) and recall (proportion of true anomalies that are flagged). A minimum precision of
0.80 and recall of 0.75 are defined as the success threshold for the combined detection
approach. F1-score will be reported as a summary metric. Separate performance breakdowns
will be provided for IQR-based statistical detection and rule-based detection to assess the
relative contribution of each component.
Baseline Comparison: The proposed system will be evaluated against two explicit baselines.
The primary baseline is a simple aggregate-duration threshold method, which flags interviews
as anomalous solely on the basis of total interview duration falling outside the 1.5 times IQR
range, representing the most common current practice in survey quality monitoring. The
secondary baseline is manual inspection using a single-feature rule (zero back-navigation flag
only), reflecting a minimal rule-based approach. Improvements in precision, recall, and F1-score
over both baselines will be reported, demonstrating the added diagnostic value of the full
multi-feature extraction pipeline.
Cross-format robustness will be evaluated by running the full pipeline on separately generated
synthetic datasets in SurveyCTO and ODK formats. If feature distributions or anomaly detection
performance differ substantially between formats, the discrepancy will be analyzed and
documented.
5.1 Synthetic Data Generation Strategy
Synthetic datasets will be generated to simulate realistic survey audit logs for both SurveyCTO
and ODK formats. This approach is well-established in evaluation contexts where labelled real-
world data is unavailable or insufficient, as it enables controlled injection of known anomaly
types and reproducible benchmarking of detection methods [11, 14]. Furthermore, synthetic
data generation is a standard practice in machine learning for handling imbalanced datasets
and evaluating detection algorithms [14]. A baseline dataset representing normal interview
behavior will be constructed using probabilistic distributions derived from literature-informed
assumptions. Specifically, log-normal distributions will be used to model response times
(consistent with empirical observations of response latency distributions in survey research [5])
and Poisson distributions will be used to model navigation event counts [11].
Anomalies will be injected systematically based on patterns identified in prior work. Fabricated
interviews will be simulated by compressing response time distributions (producing low
variance and low IQR), reducing back-navigation events to near zero, and enforcing uniform
section pacing, consistent with the patterns identified by Wenz et al. [6]. Speeding behavior will
be simulated by scaling response times below realistic thresholds, while disengagement
patterns will be represented through increased pause frequency and erratic navigation
sequences, as described by Conrad et al. [3].
Each injected anomaly type will be labelled explicitly, enabling controlled evaluation of
detection performance across different anomaly categories. The proportion of injected
anomalies will be varied across experimental conditions to assess the sensitivity of evaluation
metrics to class imbalance, addressing a known limitation of fixed-ratio synthetic evaluation
designs [11].

8. Limitations
While the proposed methodology is grounded in established survey methodology research and
validated detection approaches, several limitations should be acknowledged.
Synthetic datasets may not fully capture the variability and complexity present in real-world
survey data. Although anomalies will be injected according to empirically motivated patterns
derived from Wenz et al. [6], the absence of real labelled paradata limits the external validity of
the evaluation. This is a recognized constraint of synthetic benchmarking approaches [11], and
future work should extend the system to real paradata corpora as these become available.
Rule-based detection thresholds are derived from literature and may not generalize uniformly
across all survey instruments, contexts, or populations. Thresholds that are appropriate for a
household survey may differ from those suitable for a clinical or educational survey.
The feature taxonomy, while informed by the literature, is preliminary. Some features may
prove non-discriminative in practice, and format-specific constraints (particularly in ODK’s
coarser timing granularity) may limit the computability of certain question-level features.
The project does not incorporate ground-truth validation against known fabricated or poor-
quality interviews from real field operations, which would provide a stronger empirical basis for
evaluating detection performance.
Additionally, the proportion of anomalous interviews in synthetic datasets may not reflect real-
world class imbalance, potentially inflating evaluation metrics such as precision and recall. This
concern is partially addressed through the class imbalance sensitivity analysis described in
Section 5.1.
These limitations will be documented transparently in the final report and validation outputs,
and will inform recommendations for future work, including the extension of the system to real
paradata corpora and the incorporation of machine learning-based detection as a complement
to rule-based approaches.
9. AI-Assisted Development Statement
In accordance with the supervision framework for the 2025/2026 academic year, this section
discloses the planned use of AI-assisted tools during the development of this project.
9.1 AI Tools to be Used
The following AI tools are planned for use during the project: (1) GitHub Copilot, for in-editor
code suggestions and autocompletion during implementation of the parser and feature
extraction modules; (2) Claude (Anthropic), for reviewing draft documentation, assisting with
structuring written sections of the final report, and providing explanations of statistical
concepts; and (3) ChatGPT (OpenAI), as a secondary tool for exploratory discussion of design
decisions and debugging assistance.
9.2 Intended Tasks and Scope of Assistance
AI assistance will be limited to the following tasks: (a) boilerplate code generation for data
parsing, unit test scaffolding, and Pandas DataFrame operations, where the logic and design
remain the student’s own; (b) proofreading and improving clarity of written documentation,
without altering the analytical content or conclusions; and (c) answering clarifying questions
about library APIs and Python syntax. AI tools will not be used to generate the core algorithmic
logic of the feature extraction pipeline, the anomaly detection thresholds, or the interpretation
of evaluation results, as these are central to the academic contribution of the project.
9.3 Validation of AI-Generated Code
All AI-generated or AI-assisted code will be reviewed line-by-line before integration. Validation
will be performed through: (i) unit testing using Pytest, with test cases designed independently
of any AI suggestion; (ii) manual trace-through of logic against known input-output pairs
derived from real or synthetic audit log examples; and (iii) cross-referencing against the
relevant library documentation to confirm correctness. Any code segment that cannot be fully
understood and explained by the student will not be incorporated into the project deliverables.
9.4 Documentation of AI Usage
AI usage will be documented throughout the project in a dedicated AI Usage Log, maintained as
a separate appendix to the final report. For each instance of AI assistance, the log will record:
the tool used, the date, the task or question posed, a summary of the output received, and the
extent to which the output was used, modified, or rejected. This log will be submitted alongside
the code repository and final report, providing a transparent and auditable record of AI
involvement consistent with the supervision framework requirements.
