Abstract 
Paradata provides valuable insights into respondent and enumerator behavior during survey 
data collection, yet it remains underutilized due to the complexity of transforming raw audit 
logs into meaningful analytical features. This project proposes the design and implementation 
of an automated system for extracting timing-based and behavioral features from survey 
paradata generated by SurveyCTO and ODK platforms. The system ingests raw audit logs, 
parses platform-specific formats into a common intermediate representation, and applies a 
structured feature extraction pipeline to compute over thirty timing-based indicators spanning 
question-level, section-level, and survey-level granularities. Statistical measures - including 
entropy, variance, skewness, and kurtosis - characterize response distributions, while 
Interquartile Range (IQR) thresholding and rule-based logic identify anomalous interview 
patterns. Validation is conducted using synthetic datasets with injected anomalies, evaluated 
against precision, recall, and effect-size criteria. The outcome is a reproducible Python feature 
library, accompanying paradata format documentation, and an empirical validation report - 
providing a practical bridge between theoretical survey methodology research and scalable 
quality assurance implementation. 
 
1. Introduction 
The quality of survey data plays a critical role in research, policy formulation, and decision-
making across public health, economics, and the social sciences. Digital data collection 
platforms such as SurveyCTO and Open Data Kit (ODK) have substantially improved the 
efficiency of field survey administration. Both platforms automatically record auxiliary data - 
commonly referred to as paradata - that captures detailed information about how interviews 
are conducted rather than the responses themselves. 
Paradata includes event-level timestamps, navigation sequences, inter-keystroke intervals, 
pause durations, and back-navigation events logged throughout the interview process. These 
behavioral indicators offer diagnostic potential that extends well beyond conventional quality 
checks. Unusually fast completion times may indicate rushed or fabricated responses; 
extended pauses may signal confusion, enumerator distraction, or external interruptions; and 
atypical edit patterns may point to satisficing or straight lining behavior. 
Despite this potential, paradata is routinely underutilized in practice. Raw audit logs from 
SurveyCTO and ODK differ in structure, field naming conventions, and granularity, making 
cross-platform analysis non-trivial. Existing quality assurance approaches largely depend on 
aggregate indicators such as total interview duration or manual back-check supervision - 
methods that are poorly equipped to detect subtle behavioral irregularities at scale. 
This project addresses these challenges by developing an automated, platform-agnostic 
pipeline that extracts, structures, and analyses timing-based paradata features. By combining 
data engineering techniques with statistical anomaly detection, the system transforms raw 
logs into structured datasets suitable for quality assessment - operationalizing a body of 
survey methodology research that has hitherto remained largely theoretical. 
1.1 Problem Statement 
Existing survey quality assurance methods are limited in their ability to detect complex patterns 
of poor data collection. Most approaches rely on simple aggregate metrics - primarily total 
interview duration - that do not adequately capture behavioral irregularities during the interview process. Consequently, issues such as rushed responses, inattentive navigation, or 
fabricated data entry may go undetected. 
Although paradata contains rich behavioral information, it is stored in raw audit logs whose 
format varies across platforms. SurveyCTO produces event-based CSV logs with millisecond-
level timestamps tied to field identifiers, whereas ODK logs differ in event taxonomy and timing 
granularity. The absence of automated, format-aware tools for transforming these logs into 
analytical features creates a significant barrier for researchers and survey organizations 
seeking to operationalize paradata at scale. 
1.2 Project Aim 
The aim of this project is to design and implement an automated pipeline for extracting, 
cataloguing, and analyzing timing-based paradata features from SurveyCTO and ODK audit 
logs, in order to support scalable survey data quality assessment. 
1.3 Objectives 
1. To review and catalogue timing-based quality indicators identified in survey 
methodology literature, mapping prior findings to a structured feature taxonomy. 
2. To develop format-aware parsers for SurveyCTO and ODK audit logs that produce a 
common intermediate representation. 
3. To implement a feature extraction module computing 30-50 timing-based indicators 
across question-level, section-level, and survey-level granularities. 
4. To compute statistical characterization measures including variance, entropy, 
skewness, and kurtosis for each feature dimension. 
5. To implement anomaly detection using IQR-based thresholding and rule-based logic, 
validated against precision and recall criteria. 
6. To generate clean, structured output datasets, feature documentation, and a 
paradata format guide suitable for reuse by survey researchers.

2. Literature Review 
The Total Survey Error (TSE) framework, as developed by Groves et al. [1], provides the 
theoretical foundation for understanding data quality as the product of error sources spanning all stages of the survey process. Within this framework, paradata has emerged as a practical 
mechanism for monitoring and diagnosing error at the interviewing stage. Couper [2] 
demonstrated that process data captured within computer-assisted survey instruments could 
serve as quality indicators, establishing the methodological basis for timing-based analysis. 
Yan and Tourangeau [5] directly examined response latency as a quality indicator, finding that 
question complexity, respondent age, and familiarity with topic all predicted response time 
variation. Their work identified mean response latency and within-respondent latency variance 
as particularly discriminative features - questions answered unusually quickly relative to their 
complexity profile are associated with reduced cognitive engagement and lower data quality. 
This finding directly motivates the inclusion of question-level response time, time-to-first-
interaction, and complexity-adjusted pace features in the proposed taxonomy. 
Conrad, Tourangeau, and Couper [3] explored adaptive survey design using paradata, 
demonstrating that real-time behavioral signals - including extended pauses and repeated 
back-navigation - could identify respondent difficulty and trigger adaptive interventions. Their 
analysis underscored the relevance of section-level pause frequency, within-section timing 
variance, and back-navigation rate as indicators of processing difficulty or disengagement. 
The most directly applicable empirical work for the present project is that of Wenz, Kreuter, 
and Janke [6], who investigated data fabrication detection using paradata in the context of 
face-to-face interviewer-administered surveys. They found that fabricated interviews exhibited 
characteristically compressed response time distributions - notably reduced inter-quartile 
spread and lower variance in question-level durations - along with abnormally low back-
navigation counts and unusually uniform section pace. These findings establish the statistical 
basis for the anomaly detection component of this project and directly inform the selection of 
IQR-based thresholding as a detection method. 
Kreuter [4] provides a broader treatment of paradata-based quality improvement, surveying 
implementations across several large-scale survey programs. A recurring finding is that 
computational processing of timing data requires platform-specific parsing strategies before 
any analytical features can be extracted - a practical challenge that motivates the parser 
development component of this project. Survey-level features such as total interview duration, 
speed trajectory across the interview, and time-of-day effects are identified by Kreuter as 
robust indicators that survive aggregation across diverse survey instruments. 
Taken together, this body of work establishes a clear empirical basis for a taxonomy of timing-
based features organized across three levels of granularity. Table 1 maps the key findings 
from prior work to the features planned for extraction in this project.

 3. Proposed Methodology 
The project follows an experimental and iterative development methodology. The system is 
structured as a multi-stage pipeline progressing from raw log ingestion through parsing, 
feature extraction, statistical characterization, anomaly detection, and output generation. 
 
 
Fig. 1. System pipeline for paradata feature extraction and anomaly detection. 
 
Figure 1 illustrates the modular pipeline architecture, highlighting the separation between the 
parsing layer and the feature extraction layer. Raw audit logs from SurveyCTO and ODK enter 
at the left; platform-specific parsers normalize them into a Common Intermediate 
Representation (CIR); and the feature extraction, statistical characterization, and anomaly 
detection modules all operate exclusively on this standardized format. This separation ensures 
that adding support for a new platform requires only a new parser, without modifying 
downstream components. 
The pipeline begins with the ingestion of raw audit logs from SurveyCTO and ODK. These 
logs differ substantially in format: SurveyCTO produces event-based CSV files with 
millisecond-precision timestamps tied to field identifiers and event types (e.g., form-start, 
constraint, end), while ODK logs record a different event taxonomy with coarser timing 
granularity and distinct field naming conventions. Platform-specific parsers normalize each 
format into a common intermediate representation - a structured event table with 
standardized columns for interview ID, field name, event type, and timestamp - enabling all 
downstream processing to operate on a single data model. 
Feature engineering is applied to the intermediate representation to compute timing-based 
indicators across three levels of granularity. At the question level, the system calculates 
response time per field, time-to-first-interaction, edit count, and whether the field was revisited. 
At the section level, it aggregates section pace, within-section timing variance, pause 
frequency, and pause duration. At the survey level, it computes total interview duration, speed 
trajectory across the interview arc, back-navigation rate, and time-of-day flags. Statistical 
measures - including variance, entropy, skewness, and kurtosis - characterize the 
distributional properties of each feature across questions and across interviews.

3.2 Parser Design and Format Handling 
Both SurveyCTO and ODK are within scope, and their audit log formats require distinct parsing 
strategies before a unified analysis can proceed. SurveyCTO logs are event-based CSV files 
in which each row records a single form event with fields for event (e.g., form-start, form-
resume, constraint, end), node (the XPath field identifier), and start/end timestamps in epoch 
milliseconds. ODK audit logs record event, node, and start/end fields in a broadly similar CSV 
structure, but with a different event taxonomy - using terms such as question rather than 
field-specific events - and timestamps in ISO 8601 format rather than epoch milliseconds. 
The parser module will implement a class hierarchy in which a base ParadataParser defines 
the common intermediate schema and shared transformation logic, while subclasses 
SurveyCTOParser and ODKParser handle platform-specific ingestion and timestamp 
normalisation. The resulting intermediate representation is a standardised Pandas DataFrame 
with columns: interview_id, field_name, event_type, start_ms, end_ms, and duration_ms. All 
downstream feature extraction operates exclusively on this intermediate format, ensuring that 
new platform parsers can be added without modifying the feature library. 
3.3 Anomaly Detection 
Definition of an Anomaly 
For the purposes of this project, an anomaly is defined as an interview exhibiting statistically 
significant deviation from expected timing behavior, including one or more of the following 
criteria: 
 Extremely low or high total duration relative to the sample IQR (below Q1  1.5  IQR 
or above Q3 + 1.5  IQR) 
 Abnormally low variance in response times, indicating suspiciously uniform pacing 
across questions of differing complexity 
 Absence of natural pauses or back-navigation across more than twenty consecutive 
questions 
 Inconsistent timing patterns relative to question complexity - specifically, uniformly 
fast responses irrespective of question length or type 
 
Anomaly detection is performed at the interview level using two complementary approaches. 
Statistical flagging applies IQR-based thresholding: for each timing feature, interviews with 
values below Q1 - 1.5  IQR or above Q3 + 1.5  IQR are flagged as potential anomalies. This 
is consistent with the fabrication detection approach validated by Wenz et al. [6], whose key 
discriminative features - compressed response time IQR and low back-navigation - are 
directly captured in the feature taxonomy. 
The choice of IQR-based detection is motivated by its robustness to non-normal distributions, 
which are common in timing-based behavioral data. Unlike Z-score methods, which assume 
approximate normality, IQR-based thresholding is resistant to skewed distributions and 
extreme values. Furthermore, the project intentionally avoids machine learning-based 
approaches to maintain interpretability and alignment with survey methodology practices, 
where transparent and explainable quality indicators are preferred over black-box models. 
While robust, IQR-based methods may fail to detect multivariate anomalies where individual 
features appear normal but joint patterns are irregular.  
 
 
Fig. 2. Illustration of outlier detection using the boxplot (IQR) method. 
 
Figure 2 illustrates how the IQR method identifies outlying interviews: the central box spans 
Q1 to Q3, the whiskers extend to 1.5  IQR on each side, and points beyond the whiskers are 
flagged as statistical anomalies. This method is applied independently to each timing feature, 
with interviews receiving an anomaly score proportional to the number of features on which 
they are flagged. 
Rule-based flagging supplements statistical detection with domain-derived thresholds: 
interviews where total duration falls below a minimum plausible completion time, where back-
navigation rate is zero across more than twenty questions, or where the timing entropy of the 
response sequence falls below a defined floor are automatically flagged. These rules 
operationalize the qualitative indicators described by Kreuter [4] and Conrad et al. [3]. 
3.3.1 Computational Complexity Considerations 
The pipeline is designed with computational efficiency in mind. Feature extraction operations 
use vectorized Pandas operations with a time complexity of approximately O(n) per interview, 
where n is the number of audit log events, ensuring scalability to large datasets. The IQR-
based anomaly detection step operates in O(m log m) time over m interviews due to the sorting 
requirement, which remains tractable at the scale of typical survey datasets (hundreds to low 
thousands of interviews). Chunked processing will be applied for datasets exceeding available 
memory thresholds, with benchmarking conducted on synthetic datasets of increasing size 
(500, 2,000, and 10,000 simulated interviews) to characterize throughput and identify 
bottlenecks prior to deployment.

This project operates on paradata rather than survey response content, substantially reducing

synthetically generated, eliminating respondent privacy concerns entirely. Proper attribution 
will be given to all external sources, libraries, and prior work.

5. Evaluation Methodology 
Evaluation will proceed at two levels: feature validation and anomaly detection performance. 
Feature validation will assess whether individual features are statistically discriminative. For 
each feature, Mann-Whitney U tests will compare distributions between normal and 
anomalous interviews in the synthetic dataset. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) will be reported. 
Features with non-significant separation or negligible effect size will be documented and 
excluded from the anomaly detection stage, with the rationale recorded in the feature 
documentation. 
Anomaly detection performance will be assessed against labelled ground truth in the synthetic 
dataset. The primary metrics are precision (proportion of flagged interviews that are true 
anomalies) and recall (proportion of true anomalies that are flagged). A minimum precision of 
0.80 and recall of 0.75 are defined as the success threshold for the combined detection 
approach. F1-score will be reported as a summary metric. Separate performance breakdowns 
will be provided for IQR-based statistical detection and rule-based detection to assess the 
relative contribution of each component. 
Cross-format robustness will be evaluated by running the full pipeline on separately generated 
synthetic datasets in SurveyCTO and ODK formats. If feature distributions or anomaly 
detection performance differ substantially between formats, the discrepancy will be analyzed 
and documented. 
 
5.1 Synthetic Data Generation Strategy 
Synthetic datasets will be generated to simulate realistic survey audit logs for both 
SurveyCTO and ODK formats. A baseline dataset representing normal interview behavior 
will be constructed using probabilistic distributions derived from literature-informed 
assumptions (e.g., log-normal distributions for response times and Poisson distributions for 
navigation events). Anomalies will be injected systematically based on patterns identified in prior work. 
Fabricated interviews will be simulated by compressing response time distributions (low 
variance, low IQR), reducing back-navigation events to near zero, and enforcing uniform 
section pacing. Speeding behavior will be simulated by scaling response times below realistic 
thresholds, while disengagement patterns will be represented through increased pause 
frequency and erratic navigation. 
Each injected anomaly type will be labelled explicitly, enabling controlled evaluation of 
detection performance across different anomaly categories.

8. Limitations 
While the proposed methodology is grounded in established survey methodology research 
and validated detection approaches, several limitations should be acknowledged: 
 Synthetic datasets may not fully capture the variability and complexity present in real-
world survey data. Although anomalies will be injected according to empirically 
motivated patterns derived from Wenz et al. [6], the absence of real labelled paradata 
limits the external validity of the evaluation. 
 Rule-based detection thresholds are derived from literature and may not generalize 
uniformly across all survey instruments, contexts, or populations. Thresholds that are 
appropriate for a household survey may differ from those suitable for a clinical or 
educational survey. 
 The feature taxonomy, while informed by the literature, is preliminary. Some features 
may prove non-discriminative in practice, and format-specific constraints - 
particularly in ODK's coarser timing granularity - may limit the computability of 
certain question-level features. 
 The project does not incorporate ground-truth validation against known fabricated or 
poor-quality interviews from real field operations, which would provide a stronger 
empirical basis for evaluating detection performance. 
 Additionally, the proportion of anomalous interviews in synthetic datasets may not 
reflect real-world class imbalance, potentially inflating evaluation metrics such as 
precision and recall. 
These limitations will be documented transparently in the final report and validation outputs, 
and will inform recommendations for future work, including the extension of the system to real 
paradata corpora and the incorporation of machine learning-based detection as a complement 
to rule-based approaches.
